**TL;DR:** Good onboarding reduces avoidable uncertainty for new hires. Break
work into learnable components, define a minimum productive capability, provide
a structured path and use checklists, practice and frequent feedback. An "80%"
threshold can be a planning heuristic, not a universal law of learning.

<!--more-->

## Introduction

I recently watched a lively presentation titled
[Opinionated Onboarding](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Og7NzaVpceE), which
discussed the impact of onboarding practices on new staff and business success.
Drawing from experience, the speaker argued that poor onboarding harms companies
while good onboarding can improve productivity and retention. Having experienced
both ends of the spectrum myself, I agree with the central thesis. Companies
frequently spend months getting new staff up to speed, at a cost to both
productivity and talent.

## The Problem

New employees face a substantial cognitive burden as they simultaneously
navigate multiple learning curves: mastering the tech stack, deciphering an
unfamiliar codebase, adapting to team workflows, understanding the business
domain, and learning organisational structures. Many companies worsen this
situation by expecting staff to direct their own learning from vague
instructions such as "go learn X and tell us when you're done", or by
immediately assigning tickets without context or support. According to the
presenter, if a software developer stays at a company for 20 months but takes
six months to become productive, nearly a third of their tenure is lost[^1].
Hiring only senior professionals who can supposedly "hit the ground running"
limits the talent pool and does not remove the need for context.

## Effective Onboarding Strategies

According to the presenter, effective onboarding follows clear learning
principles. Rather than overwhelming new hires with broad or unfocused
objectives, successful onboarding programmes structure learning around two
foundations:

1. Focus on building specific capabilities rather than general "understanding"
2. Account for cognitive limitations:
   - People can only hold ~4 concepts in working memory
   - New concepts take more mental space than familiar ones
   - Skills must be practiced close to when they're learned

### Best Practices

These principles translate into concrete practices that any organisation can
implement. While the skills and technologies vary between teams, successful
onboarding programmes share common structural elements that maximise learning
efficiency while minimising cognitive overload:

- **Break down complex skills into smaller components**. Rather than asking
  someone to "learn LiveView"[^2], define tasks such as creating forms, handling
  events and managing state.
- **Provide structured learning paths**. A predefined path lets new hires focus
  on learning instead of deciding what to learn next.
- **Aim for useful proficiency before moving on**. The presenter's 80% guideline
  avoids pursuing perfection too early.
- **Define minimum productive competency**. Identify what is needed for day-one
  productivity rather than treating every skill as immediately essential.
- **Use checklists, practice and frequent feedback**. Clear checkpoints make
  progress visible, while feedback prevents incorrect habits from becoming
  established.
- **Build mechanical competency**. Once basic operations become automatic,
  developers can focus on more complex problems.

## Conclusion

Effective onboarding is more than a nice-to-have. By _breaking down_ complex
skills, providing _structured learning_ paths and _respecting_ cognitive
limitations, organisations can reduce the time it takes new hires to become
productive. This investment can also broaden hiring possibilities by making it
practical to support people who do not arrive with every skill already in place.
