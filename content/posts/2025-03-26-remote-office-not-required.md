**TL;DR:** In _Remote_, the founders of 37signals argue that distributed work
can widen recruitment and reduce commuting, provided that teams design
communication, access, trust and boundaries deliberately. The book is a useful
operating guide, not evidence that remote work increases productivity or
wellbeing for every role and person.

<!--more-->

## Introduction

Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson published _Remote: Office Not Required_
in 2013. This retrospective considers which arguments remain useful and which
details now need updating. It is a book review, not a claim that remote work
suits every person, role or organisation.

## Why Remote Work Makes Sense

### The Office Paradox

The book argues that interruption, rather than the office itself, often hinders
focused work. Meetings, impromptu conversations and noise can fragment
attention. A remote setting can reduce those interruptions, but it can also
introduce domestic constraints, unsuitable space and digital interruption. The
design goal is protected focus with accessible collaboration, wherever the work
occurs.

### The End of Commuting

Avoiding a commute can return time and reduce travel cost. The amount varies by
person and working pattern, and saved time should not silently become a longer
workday. Hybrid arrangements may retain some travel while providing in-person
contact that people value.

### Access to Global Talent

Remote work can expand recruitment beyond commuting distance. It does not
automatically create an international or diverse workforce: employment law, time
zones, language, accessibility, pay policy and biased selection still shape who
can join and succeed.

## Making Remote Work Work

### Communication: The Key to Success

Effective remote work hinges on communication. The book advocates for a blend of
synchronous and asynchronous communication methods:

1. **Overlap time**: Keep enough shared working time for conversations that
   genuinely need a quick response.
2. **Screen sharing**: Collaborate visually when explaining a problem is easier
   than describing it.
3. **Transparent documentation**: Make decisions and context available
   regardless of time zone.
4. **Virtual water cooler**: Create optional spaces for informal conversation
   and social connection.

### Navigating Legal and Financial Considerations

The book doesn't shy away from the practical challenges of remote work. In the
chapter "Taxes, accounting, laws, oh my!" the authors tackle the nuts and bolts
of remote employment:

- **Domestic remote work** can still create tax, employment and registration
  obligations across regions.
- **International remote work** adds immigration, permanent-establishment,
  payroll and worker-classification questions.
- **Contracting or employing through a local entity** involves different rights,
  costs and risks.

The book's legal discussion reflects its original publication context and should
not be treated as current advice. Employers and workers should consult qualified
professionals in the relevant jurisdictions.

### Overcoming Common Objections

Fried and Hansson systematically address the objections typically raised against
remote work:

- **"How do I know people are working?"** Define useful outcomes and make
  progress visible instead of relying on presence.
- **"What about security?"** Remote work changes the threat model and requires
  appropriate devices, access controls and practices.
- **"We need face-to-face meetings."** Many meetings can happen online, with
  occasional in-person gatherings where they add value.
- **"We need to maintain our culture."** Culture comes from repeated values and
  behaviours, not proximity alone.

### Avoiding Remote Work Pitfalls

The book doesn't gloss over remote work's challenges:

1. **Isolation**: Make social contact possible without turning it into mandatory
   surveillance or constant meetings.
2. **Overwork**: Establish boundaries and look for signs that people cannot
   disconnect.
3. **Communication barriers**: Write decisions down and choose the medium to
   match the urgency and complexity of the message.

## Building and Managing a Remote Team

### Hiring for Remote Work

The authors emphasise qualities that can help remote workers. I would frame
these as conditions a team should support rather than personality tests:

- **Agency**: People have clear goals, suitable authority and help when blocked.
- **Strong writing skills**: Clear writing is essential when much of the
  communication is asynchronous.
- **Outcome clarity**: The team agrees what useful progress looks like rather
  than relying on visible hours.

### Creating Trust and Accountability

Rather than micromanaging, successful remote teams are built on trust. The book
recommends:

- **Focus on outcomes**: Judge work by useful results rather than visible hours.
- **Use purposeful check-ins**: Brief one-to-ones can maintain connection
  without becoming burdensome.
- **Remove roadblocks**: Give remote workers the authority, information and
  access needed to be effective.

### The Remote Toolbox

The authors provide a practical "Remote Toolbox" with specific recommendations:

- **Project records**: Keep tasks, decisions and files in a shared system.
- **Video calls**: Reserve synchronous calls for conversations that benefit from
  immediate interaction.
- **Screen sharing**: Use it for collaborative diagnosis and demonstrations.
- **File sharing and collaborative documents**: Make current material available
  across locations.
- **Co-working spaces**: Offer an alternative when a home office is isolating or
  unsuitable.

Many of these tools have evolved since the book's publication, but the core
functions they serve remain essential to remote work.

### The Importance of Meetups

Despite advocating for remote work, the authors stress the value of occasional
in-person gatherings. At their company, they met at least twice yearly for 4-5
days[^1]. These meetups strengthen personal bonds, allow for intensive
collaboration, and reinforce company culture.

## Conclusion

_Remote_ remains valuable because it turns distributed work into a set of
operating questions rather than a perk: How is context recorded? Which work
needs real-time contact? How are boundaries protected? How do people receive
feedback and remain included?

Some examples and legal assumptions have aged, and the book occasionally treats
remote work's advantages too confidently. The durable lesson is not that the
future must be remote. It is that location should be an explicit design
decision, supported by communication, access, trust and suitable working
conditions.

---

[^1]: Reasonable adjustments must be considered for those who are not able to
    travel far due to health, family or other reasons beyond their control. It
    is possible to build and maintain a strong culture that does not necessitate
    travelling, or at least not travelling often or far, if circumstances don't
    allow.
