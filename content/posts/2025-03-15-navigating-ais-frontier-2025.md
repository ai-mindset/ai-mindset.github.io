**TL;DR:** Despite rapid progress in 2025, long-running AI agents remained
vulnerable to compounding decision, implementation and context errors. Better
data, evaluation, safeguards, user experience and explicit points for human
intervention mattered more than autonomy alone.

<!--more-->

## Introduction

At the 2025 AI Engineer Summit, Grace Isford, a partner at Lux Capital, gave a
[keynote](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS5a8VIKsvA) on "The State of the AI
Frontier". The talk set rapid model and infrastructure progress against the
practical failures of long-running agents. This article records that 2025
snapshot and extracts design questions that remain useful.

## The Perfect Storm for AI Agents

The speaker began by acknowledging the remarkable progress in AI over the past
two and a half years. The industry has seen exponential advancements since the
release of Stable Diffusion in August 2022, with the pace of innovation only
accelerating. 2025 has already witnessed several landmark developments:

- The announcement of the Stargate infrastructure project involving OpenAI,
  SoftBank and Oracle
- Strong results from OpenAI's o3 model on the ARC-AGI benchmark
- DeepSeek's R1 release and rapid rise in app rankings
- New European AI investment initiatives announced around the France AI Summit

These developments, alongside other factors, have created what many call the
"perfect storm" for AI agents:

1. Reasoning models such as OpenAI's o1 and o3, DeepSeek's R1, and Grok's
   contemporary offering
2. Increased test-time compute, allocating more resources to inference rather
   than only training
3. Engineering and hardware optimisations driving efficiency
4. Cheaper inference and hardware costs
5. A narrowing gap on some benchmarks between open-weight and closed models
6. Massive infrastructure investments from governments and corporations

## The Reality Gap: Why AI Agents Aren't Quite Working Yet

Despite this promising landscape, Isford argued that truly autonomous AI agents
aren't functioning as seamlessly as industry hype suggests. To illustrate this
point, she shared a real-world example of trying to use OpenAI's operator to
book a flight from New York to San Francisco with specific requirements. Despite
seemingly straightforward criteria (departure time after 3 PM, avoiding rush
hour, specific airlines, budget constraints, seat preferences), the agent failed
to deliver a satisfactory result.

The presenter identified five categories of cumulative errors that prevent AI
agents from delivering consistent, reliable results:

1. **Decision Errors**: Choosing incorrect facts or overthinking a scenario
2. **Implementation Errors**: Encountering access issues or integration failures
3. **Heuristic Errors**: Applying the wrong criteria or missing contextual
   information
4. **Taste Errors**: Failing to account for preferences that were not explicitly
   stated
5. **Perfection Paradox**: Strong performance in one area raises expectations,
   making ordinary delays or basic mistakes feel worse

Errors can compound in multi-step tasks. The presentation illustrates this with
$0.99^{50} \approx 61\%$ and $0.95^{50} \approx 8\%$. That calculation assumes
50 independent steps with the same success probability and that every step must
succeed. Real trajectories have correlated errors, recovery and steps of unequal
importance, so the figures explain the intuition rather than measure an agent.

## Five Strategies for Building Better AI Agents

The keynote then shifted to offering concrete strategies for mitigating these
challenges and building more effective AI agents:

### 1. Data Curation

- Recognise that data is increasingly diverse, including text, images, video,
  audio and sensor data
- Curate proprietary data, including data generated by the agent itself
- Design feedback loops that improve agent performance through user interactions
- Learn from explicit feedback without retaining more user data than the product
  needs

### 2. Robust Evaluation Systems

- Move beyond verifiable domains such as maths and science by developing
  frameworks for subjective assessments
- Collect signals about human preferences
- Build personalised evaluation systems that reflect actual user needs
- Use direct human testing where benchmarks do not represent the real task

### 3. Scaffolding Systems

- Implement safeguards to prevent cascading failures when errors occur
- Build complex compound systems that can work together harmoniously
- Incorporate human intervention at critical junctures
- Detect selected failures, retry within bounds and escalate when recovery is
  uncertain

### 4. User Experience as a Competitive Moat

- Recognise that UX differentiation matters when many applications use the same
  foundation models
- Deeply understand user workflows to create elegant human-machine collaboration
- Integrate seamlessly with existing systems to deliver tangible ROI
- Focus on domains with proprietary data or specialised workflows

### 5. Multimodal Approaches

- Move beyond basic chatbot interfaces to create more human-like experiences
- Incorporate multiple modalities such as vision and voice
- Use bounded, user-visible memory only where it improves the task and consent
  is clear
- Use new interfaces only where they improve the underlying task

## Conclusion

While 2025 has created what appears to be a perfect storm for AI agents with
advanced reasoning models, increased compute efficiency, and massive
infrastructure investments, the reality is that autonomous AI agents still face
significant challenges. The cumulative effect of small errors across
decision-making, implementation, heuristics, and user preferences creates
substantial reliability issues in complex agent systems.

The practical lesson is to design for partial reliability rather than assume
autonomy. Curated data, task-specific evaluations, safeguards, usable interfaces
and human intervention can make agents valuable even when a fully autonomous
system would be too brittle.
