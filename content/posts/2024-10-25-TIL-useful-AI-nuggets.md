**TL;DR:** Notes from AI practitioners point to a grounded set of advantages:
engineering skill, adaptability, careful project selection, direct inspection of
data, clear communication and enough rest to think well. None guarantees a
breakthrough, but each improves the quality and speed of feedback.

<!--more-->

## Introduction

While working through Stanford's
[CS25: Transformers United](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs25/), I collected
advice that reached beyond model architecture. The original remarks came largely
from Jason Wei. Here is what I think remains useful after removing the slogans
and large multipliers.

## Engineering and Adaptability

Wei contrasted an older emphasis on credentials and narrow publication records
with the growing value of
[engineering ability and adaptation](https://x.com/_jasonwei/status/1631017964286922753).
The contrast is deliberately sharp, but the practical point is sound: research
ideas become more useful when you can implement, measure and revise them.

Academic depth and engineering skill are not substitutes. The most capable teams
often need both.

## Project Choice Sets the Ceiling

The [problem you choose](https://x.com/_jasonwei/status/1514327894746574851)
constrains the value of even excellent execution. Before optimising a model or
pipeline, ask:

- Who benefits if this works?
- What decision will the result change?
- What is the cheapest evidence that the problem is real?
- Which assumption is most likely to invalidate the work?

These questions do not guarantee a good project, but they can expose a weak one
early.

## Inspect the Data

One recurring theme is the willingness to
[inspect many examples directly](https://x.com/_jasonwei/status/1708921475829481683)
and to build tools that make inspection quick. Aggregate metrics can hide
duplicated records, label ambiguity, spurious correlations and systematic
failure modes. Looking at samples is not a substitute for measurement; it tells
you what else needs measuring.

## Communication Is Technical Work

Wei identifies
[prioritisation and communication](https://x.com/_jasonwei/status/1689346627428036608)
as important research skills. A clear experiment note, a well-framed question or
an honest account of uncertainty can prevent more wasted effort than another
layer of infrastructure.

The same principle applies to management. Asking for specific feedback and
discussing constraints early can work well in a healthy organisation. It is not
universally safe advice, so trust and context still matter.

## Study How the Field Changes

A fast-moving field rewards awareness of new work, but chasing every release
produces shallow knowledge. A better approach is to study the pattern of change:
which ideas persist, which bottlenecks move and which old techniques become
useful under new constraints.

Informal write-ups and experiment logs help here because they reveal the path to
a result, including discarded approaches that a polished paper may omit.

## Protect the Ability to Think

Health advice can become simplistic quickly. The defensible point is modest:
sleep, recovery and sustainable working habits affect judgement. Working longer
cannot compensate indefinitely for choosing the wrong question or running an
experiment you do not understand.

## Conclusion

The common thread is feedback quality. Choose problems carefully, build enough
to test them, inspect the evidence, explain what happened and preserve the
capacity to repeat the cycle. That is less dramatic than a promise of 10x or
100x output, but far more useful as a working practice.
