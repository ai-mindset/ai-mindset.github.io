**TL;DR:** The BitNet b1.58 paper trained models from scratch with ternary
weights in {-1, 0, 1} and 8-bit activations. In the authors' 3B-parameter
comparison, the model matched their full-precision baseline while using 3.55x
less GPU memory and producing tokens 2.71x faster. Its 71.4x energy figure is an
estimate for matrix-multiplication arithmetic on a 7nm process, not a measured
reduction in total system energy.

## Introduction

In February 2024, researchers released
[_The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits_](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17764).
Its central idea is to train a Transformer whose linear-layer weights take only
three values. That replaces much of the floating-point multiplication in those
layers with cheaper integer arithmetic, although complete inference still
includes higher-precision operations and memory movement.

## Contributions

BitNet b1.58 replaces linear layers with `BitLinear`, uses ternary weights and
quantises activations to 8 bits. It is a quantisation-aware architecture trained
from scratch, not a drop-in post-training conversion for any existing model.

The paper reports that its 3B model matched the reproduced FP16 baseline on
perplexity and a small suite of zero-shot tasks. Under the authors' kernels and
hardware setup, it used 3.55x less GPU memory and had 2.71x lower per-token
latency. Larger-model results included projected or system-specific gains, so
they should not be read as universal figures for every runtime or device.

## What Does 1.58-bits Mean?

Three possible states require $\log_2(3)$ bits of information when they are
equally likely:

$$ -(\frac{1}{3} \log_2(\frac{1}{3}) + \frac{1}{3} \log_2(\frac{1}{3}) + \frac{1}{3} \log_2(\frac{1}{3})) = -(3 × (\frac{1}{3} \log_2(\frac{1}{3}))) = -\log_2(\frac{1}{3}) \approx 1.58496... bits $$

## Conclusion

The paper provides evidence that aggressively quantised models trained for the
representation can approach a comparable full-precision baseline at the tested
scale. It also identifies the implementation challenge: theoretical arithmetic
savings become practical gains only when kernels and hardware support the
representation efficiently.

The result is promising for memory-constrained inference and specialised
hardware. It is not evidence that an arbitrary full-precision model can be
compressed to 1.58 bits without retraining or that total deployment energy will
fall by the paper's arithmetic-only estimate.
