**TL;DR:** Despite major advances in science and technology, antibiotic
development declined after the post-war "Golden Age" of the 1940s-1960s.
Scientific capability alone is insufficient without economic incentives,
institutional coordination and systematic use of new discovery tools.

<!--more-->

## Introduction

An
[Our World in Data analysis](https://ourworldindata.org/golden-age-antibiotics)[^1]
describes the rapid development of antibiotic classes from the early 1940s to
the mid-1960s. In the dataset it reproduces, almost two-thirds of antibiotic
drug classes were introduced during that period, and most remain in use[^2].\
The same dataset counted eight new classes approved from 1970 through 2023.
Counts can depend on the definition of a class and the approving jurisdiction,
but the slowdown is clear. Traditional organism screening also became less
productive as researchers repeatedly encountered known compounds[^3].\
Genome sequencing, synthetic biology and new cultivation methods expand the
search space. Research suggests that only a fraction of bacterial species have
been identified, some of which may produce useful compounds[^2]. Moving a
candidate from discovery to an approved medicine still requires evidence of
safety and efficacy, manufacturing, stewardship and a viable funding model. This
article examines this paradoxical inverse relationship between technological
capability and antibiotic development: How did the Golden Age achieve such
remarkable success with limited tools? Why has progress slowed as our
capabilities have grown? Most importantly, what combinations of economic
incentives and modern technology could spark a new era of antibiotic discovery?

## When Urgency Met Innovation

The Golden Age of Antibiotics stands as medicine's most productive period in
antimicrobial discovery, yielding over 20 new antibiotic classes - more than
double what we've developed in the 50 years since[^2]. Three pivotal
breakthroughs, coupled with unprecedented coordination, drove this remarkable
success.\
The foundation was laid by Paul Ehrlich's systematic approach to drug discovery.
By methodically testing hundreds of compounds, his team discovered
[salvarsan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arsphenamine) in 1909 - an early
synthetic antimicrobial treatment for syphilis[^2]. A second milestone emerged
when Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928. However, scaling
penicillin required a coordinated wartime effort. With infections being the
second-most common cause of hospital admissions in the US Army, the U.S. Office
of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD) helped organise a search for more
productive penicillin strains, including a high-yielding strain found on a
cantaloupe[^4].\
The third breakthrough came from work on soil bacteria. Selman Waksman
identified the potential of
[actinomycetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actinomycetales), and Waksman and
his doctoral student Albert Schatz discovered
[streptomycin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptomycin) through systematic
screening[^5]. The work opened an important avenue for antibiotic discovery.
What transformed these breakthroughs into a "golden age" was unusual
coordination. Government, academia and industry shared data and manufacturing
knowledge under wartime pressure, accelerating development and production[^6].

## Scientific Progress and Market Failure

The contrast between the Golden Age and our current era reflects a fundamental
misalignment between public health needs and market incentives[^2]. The market
structure fundamentally disfavours antibiotics in two ways:

1. **Revenue structure**: Medicines for chronic conditions can generate revenue
   over long periods, whereas an antibiotic is usually prescribed for a short
   course. This gap has contributed to companies leaving antibiotic
   development[^7][^8].
2. **Conservation requirements**: New antibiotics should be reserved for
   infections that need them. Appropriate stewardship is essential for public
   health but limits sales volume[^7].

Meanwhile, our technological capabilities offer three particularly promising
approaches:

1. **Genome mining**: Identify biosynthetic gene clusters that remain silent
   under standard laboratory conditions. This approach has yielded candidates
   such as humimycins[^9].
2. **Environmental exploration**: Study microbes from under-sampled environments
   and organisms that are difficult to culture[^3].
3. **Combination strategies**: Exploit cases where resistance to one antibiotic
   creates vulnerability to another[^10].

These tools have not yet restored the earlier rate of new-class introduction.
Scientific difficulty, trial attrition, stewardship and weak commercial
incentives all contribute; reducing the explanation to one cause would be
misleading.

## Integrating Economics and Technology

Drawing from evidence in antibiotic development research, several promising
approaches could help overcome current market failures while leveraging modern
technological capabilities[^7].

### Economic Solutions to Market Failures

1. **Subscription models**: Pay for access to an antibiotic rather than
   rewarding sales volume, separating revenue from use[^7].
2. **Advance market commitments**: Promise payments for products that meet
   defined requirements, reducing uncertainty for developers[^11].
3. **Collaborative funding**: Initiatives such as CARB-X and GARDP share the
   cost and risk of development[^7].

### Leveraging Modern Technology

To maximise the impact of these economic incentives, three technological
approaches show particular promise:

1. **Systematic genome mining**: Identify promising antibiotic-producing genes
   that traditional screening could miss[^9].
2. **Environmental exploration**: Use sequencing and cultivation techniques to
   search a broader range of microbial life[^3].
3. **Combination strategies**: Test how resistance to one treatment changes
   susceptibility to another[^10].

## Conclusion

The story of antibiotic development demonstrates that scientific capability
alone cannot drive progress. The Golden Age succeeded through a powerful
combination of systematic approaches, unprecedented collaboration, and removal
of institutional barriers - even with limited technological tools[^2].\
Today's challenge is fundamentally different. We possess sophisticated tools -
from genome mining to advanced screening methods - yet development has stalled.
This paradox reveals that progress requires three key elements working in
concert: economic incentives, institutional coordination, and technological
application[^7].\
The evidence-based solutions presented in the original Our World in Data article[^1]
offer a path forward. Market reforms like subscription models and advance market
commitments could help correct the fundamental economic misalignment in
antibiotic development[^8]. Meanwhile, systematic application of computational
tools, genomic analysis, and bacterial exploration could help unlock new classes
of antibiotics that traditional methods miss[^9].\
The urgency is clear. Antimicrobial resistance threatens to undermine many
advances in modern medicine[^12]. However, by combining proven coordination
approaches from the Golden Age with modern capabilities and sustainable economic
frameworks, we can revitalise antibiotic development for the challenges ahead.
